Seating guide

How to make a wedding seating chart

The spreadsheet of table numbers is the wrong first document. The list of names is the first document. Everything that goes on a wall, a tent card, or an escort card should be generated from that list after it has been seated — not typed again.

This is the order that survives a 150-person room. It is also the order TIKTAK is built around, because the other order — pick tables, then pour names into them — is how families get split and two people who must not sit together end up at table 9.

1. Get the names in, not the table numbers

Start from the RSVP list. One row per person. If your sheet says “Cohen, 4”, that is four guests, not one line with a number in it. Keep a party or household column if you have one — “bride’s parents”, “college friends”, “table of cousins” — even if you are not sure yet which table they will get.

Do not assign table numbers in the spreadsheet. A table number written next to a name becomes a second source of truth, and the second source of truth is the one that drifts. The seating chart, the escort cards, and the place cards all need the same assignment. That only happens if there is one assignment.

2. Group people who arrive together

A couple, a family with children, a set of grandparents who will not be split across the room: treat them as a unit before you place anyone. Consecutive seats around a table are neighbours. That is what “sit together” has to mean, or the party ends up facing each other across a centrepiece with strangers in between.

If someone joins a household that already exists, add them to that household. Do not make a second party with the same surname. Two parties called “Levi” is how you get two tables both claiming the Levis.

3. Lay out the room, then lock what must not move

Put the tables on a floor plan that matches the venue: rounds, long rectangles, a head table, a sweetheart table if you are using one. Seat counts have to match the furniture you actually hired. A 60-inch round takes eight comfortably and ten if you squeeze — ten is a decision, not a default.

Seat the couple, the parents, and anyone else whose place is political, then lock those seats. Auto-arrange will not touch a locked guest. If you skip the lock, the next pass of “just try again” will move the top table to make the rest of the room prettier, and you will spend the evening putting it back.

4. Mark who must sit together and who must not

Two rules are enough. “Together” means the same table. “Apart” means not the same table. You do not need a graph of every friendship. You need the handful of pairs that would ruin a dinner: a divorced pair, two colleagues in a fight, the uncle who will hold court if seated with the teenagers.

Unseated people are not yet a problem. A rule is only broken when both people have a table and that table is wrong. That is useful: you can import the whole list, mark the landmines, and seat in batches without a wall of false alarms.

The usual failure is assigning seats before the rules You place 120 people by eye, then remember the two who cannot share a table. One of them is in a party of five. Moving that party leaves a hole of five, which you fill with people who were fine where they were. The chart takes another hour. Mark the constraints first. Then sit people.

5. Fill the remaining seats

Once the locked people and the constraints are in, the rest is packing: parties onto tables that still have a run of consecutive empty chairs, without splitting a household and without seating two “apart” guests together. A computer is faster at this than a highlighter. A person is still better at the last twenty names, because those are the ones with a story.

If the room is too small, you will have people left unseated. That is the answer, not a bug. Add a table or drop a seat before you start swapping at random.

6. Print from the plan, once

When the floor plan is right, the things you hang and hand out should be exports of it: a chart for the door, an A–Z list, escort cards in surname order, place cards in seat order around each table. Change one seat and print again. Do not keep a parallel Word file of names.

Questions people ask

Do I need assigned seats, or only assigned tables?
Tables are enough if the room is casual and the tables are small. Assigned seats earn their keep at a plated dinner, a long head table, and anywhere a place card is already going down. If you print place cards, you have assigned seats whether you meant to or not.
Should kids sit with parents?
Young children, yes — they are a party. A teenagers’ table works when they already know each other and you still want a parent in eyeshot, not when you use it to park every child in the room.
How early should this be finished?
After the last RSVP you are willing to wait for, and before the cards go to print. The plan can change in an afternoon. Reprinting 160 tent cards cannot.
What if someone does not come?
Unassign them and, if you have already printed, pull the escort card before the doors open. Leave the place setting; an empty chair is quieter than a reshuffle at the door.
